Core Thesis
The AI buildout is fundamentally a power infrastructure problem. Every hyperscaler, fund, and co-location developer is racing for the same scarce resource — grid capacity. Whoever secures reliable, high-voltage power at scale, years ahead of demand, wins.
This roadmap covers the full lifecycle of developing an AI-ready data center in the United States — from site selection through to revenue — with deep coverage of the power bottleneck, supply chain constraints, and the two primary monetization paths: Colocation and GPU-as-a-Service.
Phase 1 Site & Power Strategy
01
Choose Your State
Market structure, grid reliability, and regulatory climate define your ceiling
›

State selection is the highest-leverage decision in the process. Grid structure, permitting speed, tax incentives, power cost, and available land all vary dramatically — and none can be changed later.

⚡ Grid Structure
Deregulated markets (ERCOT, PJM) allow direct power purchase agreements and often have faster interconnect timelines. Regulated states require working through the utility monopoly.
🏛 Regulatory Climate
Texas, Georgia, and Nevada are developer-friendly. Some states require Environmental Impact Statements (EIS) that can add 1–2 years. Check zoning pre-entitlements.
💰 Tax Incentives
Texas Chapter 313 abatements, data center sales tax exemptions on equipment (30+ states), and property tax abatements can reduce all-in cost by 15–25%.
🌬 Power Cost
All-in power cost ($/MWh) drives operating margins more than any other single factor. West Texas wind + solar can reach $25–$35/MWh, vs. $65–$90 in constrained Eastern markets.

02
Find the Right Land
Proximity to substations, transmission corridors, fiber, and flood-safe ground — all simultaneously
›

The ideal parcel sits within 1–3 miles of a 345kV+ substation on a clear transmission corridor, with redundant fiber access, confirmed water rights, and FEMA Zone X flood status. All four criteria must be met — missing any one effectively eliminates the site for institutional-scale development.

⚡ Power Proximity
Within 2 miles of a 345kV+ substation with confirmed capacity headroom. Informal capacity check before executing LOI. Each mile of line extension adds $1–3M in upgrade cost.
🔌 Transmission Access
Adjacent to a major transmission corridor. Line extensions >5 miles are often prohibitively expensive ($5–15M+) and add 12–18 months to the interconnection timeline.
🌐 Fiber Redundancy
Minimum 2 diverse carrier routes (Zayo, Lumen, Crown Castle). Single-carrier exposure is a non-starter for hyperscaler SLAs. Check distance to nearest carrier POPs.
🌊 Flood / Geotechnical
FEMA Zone X required. Avoid Zone A or AE. Also screen for seismic risk (western US) and subsidence (Gulf Coast). Geotech report is a Day 30 deliverable.

Step-by-Step Site Screening Workflow

Regional Screening
Open EIA Atlas → identify states with high transmission density + renewable generation. Filter to ERCOT, PJM West, or MISO. Shortlist 3–5 metro areas.
Locate Substations
HIFLD Substations → filter 345kV+ → buffer 3 miles → cross-reference land databases. Look for 50–500 acre industrial parcels with clear title.
Cross-Validate Land
Shortlist 5–10 parcels overlapping the substation buffer → verify size, zoning, ownership, and prior environmental issues (Phase I ESA).
Informal Capacity Check
Call utility interconnection group (not sales) to get informal read on substation headroom. This is free and typically takes 1–2 weeks. Do before LOI.
Confirm Fiber & Flood
Run fiber carrier check (Zayo, Lumen, Crown Castle) + pull FEMA flood map. Both must be clean before executing LOI and committing capital.

Phase 2 Permitting & Regulatory Approvals
04
Permitting & Regulatory Approvals
Five permit categories — all must close before breaking ground
›

Permitting runs in parallel with interconnection but rarely finishes faster. Community opposition and environmental review are the most common causes of slippage. Run all five tracks concurrently with separate workstreams.

🏗 Conditional Use Permit
Local zoning board approval for industrial / data center use. Requires public hearings. Budget 6–18 months and hire a local land use attorney from Day 1.
🌿 Environmental Review
NEPA or state equivalent. Water discharge, noise, visual impact. Required for most ≥20 MW facilities. Engage consultants before filing — it cuts 30–40% off the timeline.
🔥 Fire & Life Safety
Fire suppression design (pre-action or FM-200), emergency access roads, battery (BESS / UPS) storage permits. Early AHJ (Authority Having Jurisdiction) coordination is critical.
💧 Water & Discharge
Cooling tower water draw and NPDES discharge permits. Air-cooled designs reduce exposure but add cost at scale. Water rights purchase may be required in western states.
💡 Interconnection Agreement
The signed interconnection agreement with the utility is the legal milestone that locks in your power. Treat this as a parallel permit track, not a Phase 3 task. Lenders require it before funding construction debt.
Fastest TimelineTexas, Nevada: 6–12 months with experienced land use counsel
Slowest TimelineCalifornia, New York: 24–48 months common due to CEQA / SEQRA + local opposition
Community RiskNoise from cooling units and visual impact are top community objections — address proactively in CUP filings

Phase 3 Development & Financing
05
Facility Design
MEP engineering, cooling architecture, and redundancy tiers for AI-density workloads
›

AI workloads demand fundamentally different facility design than traditional enterprise data centers. GPU racks running 30–100kW require liquid cooling infrastructure, heavier structural floor loads, and distribution systems that air-cooled designs cannot support.

⚡ Power Density
30–100 kW/rack for GPU clusters (vs. 5–10 kW traditional). Floor must handle 1,000–2,000 lbs/sq ft. Standard raised-floor tiles collapse under GPU server weight.
❄ Cooling Architecture
Direct Liquid Cooling (DLC) or rear-door heat exchangers required above 30 kW/rack. Air cooling alone is physically insufficient — plan chilled water loops and CDU manifolds from Day 1 of design.
🔁 Redundancy (Tier III)
2N power (dual utility feeds + generators), N+1 UPS, N+1 cooling for Tier III SLA (99.982% uptime). Hyperscalers require Tier III minimum; AI training clusters often accept Tier II for cost savings.
📐 PUE Target
Modern AI data centers target PUE 1.2–1.3 (vs. legacy 1.6–2.0). Every 0.1 PUE improvement = ~10% reduction in power cost at scale. Critical for competitiveness on GPU-as-a-Service economics.
Long-Lead: TransformersOrder at design completion — 52–104 week lead time (see Step 8)
Long-Lead: Switchgear12–18 months. Order simultaneously with transformers — one does not work without the other
Architect SelectionUse firms with ≥3 hyperscale AI campus completions. Critical Path: Arcadis, DLR Group, HOK, Corgan
Design DurationSchematic → Design Development → Construction Documents: 12–18 months for a 100+ MW campus

Construction & Supply Chain
Three equipment bottlenecks that will delay your schedule if not ordered at design, not at groundbreaking
›

Construction itself is not the constraint — it's the electrical equipment. The global supply chain for power transformers, switchgear, and generators is under unprecedented stress from simultaneous demand by EV infrastructure, grid hardening, renewables, and AI data centers.

⚠ Bottleneck 1 — Power Transformers
2-year lead time (52–104 weeks). ABB, Siemens, GE transformer backlogs now extend beyond 104 weeks for large units. Order at or before design completion — ordering at groundbreaking is already too late by 12+ months.
⚠ Bottleneck 2 — Main Switchgear & PDUs
12–18 month lead time. Medium-voltage switchgear (15kV class) and high-density power distribution units are constrained. Eaton, Schneider Electric, and ABB all report extended backlogs. Standardize specs early to allow substitution between manufacturers.
⚠ Bottleneck 3 — Backup Generators
9–15 month lead time. Large diesel generators (2MW+) for N+1 critical backup are delayed by engine block and alternator shortages. Caterpillar and Cummins are prioritizing fleet customers. Consider natural gas alternatives where available.
Civil / Shell12–18 months from groundbreaking for shell and core
MEP Fit-Out6–12 months after shell completion
Total (Ground → CO)24–36 months typical for a 20–50 MW first phase — if equipment was ordered on time
GC SelectionTurner, Mortenson, DPR, Holder — all have dedicated data center practices. Require ≥3 hyperscale data center completions in last 5 years.

Install IT Infrastructure
GPU cluster deployment — the full AI hardware stack from silicon fab to fiber
›

Modern AI clusters are a tightly integrated stack of compute, memory packaging, optical networking, and precision liquid cooling. Each layer has its own supply chain and lead time — all must arrive and be staged in sequence. GPU allocation from NVIDIA is often the binding constraint.

6Optical Networking 400G/800G transceivers, spine-leaf topology, InfiniBand NDR or RoCE v2 fabric for GPU-to-GPU communication within the cluster
5Liquid Cooling Direct Liquid Cooling (DLC) manifolds, rear-door heat exchangers, Coolant Distribution Units (CDUs) — mandatory above 30 kW/rack
4Server Nodes 8× GPU compute nodes (H100 SXM5 / H200 / B200 / GB200 NVL72), NVLink switch boards, high-density 277V power shelves

Integrated system testing, tenant acceptance, and the moment billing begins
›

Commissioning validates every system performs to specification under full load. For hyperscale tenants, this includes their own 30–60 day acceptance protocol before Rent Commencement Date (RCD) — the contractual trigger for billing. RCD is locked at lease execution, not at acceptance.
